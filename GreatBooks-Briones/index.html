ACTIVITY 1

Flicker, Fade, Gone

Question-and-answer response

1. Could you relate to the protagonist? Why or why not?

Yes. Sometimes I too feel invisible to other people, and I feel like I don’t belong. I use other mediums, like video games and reading, just to escape reality, so that’s why I relate to this story.

2. Why did the boy disappear at the end of the story?

Maybe he realized that his crush didn’t notice him and left because of that, or because people only saw him as a key for the arcade. Rather than being remembered as a person, he was remembered as the “key” guy.

3. Did the ending surprise you? Why or why not?

Yes. There was no mention of what happened to him. He just disappeared, and people did not see him; they just saw him like a background character, which is frustrating. He did not have any development in his life.

4. What does the story seem to say about the difference between living and existing?

Living means you are involved in something, having a purpose and having an impact on others, while existing is just having a routine, like being on airplane mode and just breathing air.

5. Why did the boy end up the way he did?

He ends up the way he is because of video games. He is engrossed in the game. Instead of living, he is close to just existing. He uses video games to escape reality instead of facing the real world.

6. There are two central symbols in the story. What are they? What do they mean?

Key: it represents the power to open one’s door and his identity as a key holder.

Mirror: his desire to disappear from reality, and to fade into the world that he wants.

7. What is a Toadstool? What does the code name “Princess Toadstool” allude to?

A toadstool is a kind of mushroom. When we say “Toadstool,” it is the princess from Super Mario, the character who needs saving, and in his case, it is his crush.

8. Explain the significance of the title.

Flicker: struggling to make a decision.

Fade: slowly losing his connection to the real world.

Gone: his decision to disappear into his game.

9. Does the boy in the story remind you of anyone? Does it seem to be a commentary on certain kinds of people?

Yes, the boy reminds me of people who struggle to connect with other people, people who are only social in video games and seem comfortable only there all the time. This makes them want to completely withdraw from reality.

10. Princess Toadstool does not fall in love with the narrator. What does this tell us about the difference between fantasy and reality?

In the story, fantasy makes your world easy, with no problem. You can be the hero and save princesses. In reality, not everything is like that; if you want to be rewarded, you have to put work into it. That’s reality.
ACTIVITY 2

Ang Huling El Bimbo

Question-and-answer response

1. How does the friendship of Joy, Hector, Emman, and Anthony change from their college years to their adulthood?

From the time Joy was assaulted, the three men abandoned her, leaving her feeling alone. Even if Joy calls for them, they flat-out refuse to even listen to her. It’s sad to see that they changed for the worse.

2. What is the significance of the title Ang Huling El Bimbo?

I think the word “bimbo” is like a word for “fool.” It is like “the last fool,” or one of the other terms I hear, “the last whore,” so the title kind of describes the musical.

3. How does the musical portray the role of society in shaping a person’s choices and identity?

In the play, it shows that the poor have it rough. In Joy’s situation, she was already struggling. She was assaulted, driven to poverty and drugs, and was a case for a hit-and-run. In her story, she was used by people for their own benefit, like the man who became a konsehal, while her friends grew up in a good environment. Joy was already struggling.

4. If you were one of Joy’s three friends, what would you have done differently after the tragic incident?

I would stand by her and help her against the assaulters who attacked her. I know that in the 1990s things were different, but I would still ask for help by calling the authorities. In the play, her friends kind of abandoned her. If Joy needed someone to talk to, I would talk to her. Maybe by talking to her, I could somehow help her change the way she does things and stop what she is doing. That’s what I would do.

5. What is the most important lesson that the play Ang Huling El Bimbo teaches about friendship, responsibility, and making choices?

In Ang Huling El Bimbo, the main character was passive throughout the play. As I mentioned, she was assaulted and driven to poverty. She did not choose to change. She kept following the guy who told her to sell herself and sell drugs. I think she should have chosen differently. Her friends should have helped her and comforted her, but they did not take responsibility and kept it to themselves. I think the biggest lessons are to make the right choices and choose your friends well, and don’t go out at night so that nothing will happen.
ACTIVITY 3 · MULTIPLE LENSES

Hope for the Flowers

by Trina Paulus

Literary device: Symbolism / Allegory

Every character stands for something outside the story. The pillar of caterpillars is the rat race: status, competition, climbing over people to get ahead. The cocoon is the risk of doing nothing, of trusting a process you can’t see working. The butterfly is what’s on the other side of that risk: a self that finally has a purpose bigger than “get to the top.”

Formalist Lens

Evidence: Stripe reaches the top of the pillar and finds nothing there but more pillars, all just as crowded.

Explanation: The story repeats the same image of caterpillars climbing over each other at every pillar, not just Stripe’s. That repetition is what turns “reaching the top” into a letdown instead of a payoff. It’s a pattern doing the work, not a stated moral.

Archetypal Lens

Evidence: Yellow leaves the pillar, goes into the cocoon, and comes out as a butterfly.

Explanation: This matches the Rebirth archetype directly: something that looks like an ending (disappearing into a cocoon) turns out to be a transformation. Leaving the pillar with no guarantee of what’s next also fits the Quest archetype; she goes without proof it will work.

Marxist Lens

Evidence: Caterpillars step on and crush each other to get higher on the pillar, competing for a “top” none of them has actually seen.

Explanation: Power here is purely positional; height is the only currency, and caterpillars get exploited (literally stepped on) by whoever is climbing above them. Nobody on the pillar stops to ask why it exists, which is its own kind of answer to whether the story questions the system or lets it stand unquestioned.

Psychoanalytic Lens

Evidence: Stripe keeps climbing even after he starts to care about Yellow, caught between staying with her and going back to the pillar.

Explanation: His climbing looks driven more by fear of falling behind than by any desire he could actually name, which is what the lens asks you to look for. The pull between competing and staying with Yellow reads as a conflict between the id, the drive to win, and a more reflective, relationship-focused part of him.

Ecocritical Lens

Evidence: As caterpillars, Stripe and Yellow eat leaves to survive. As butterflies, they pollinate flowers.

Explanation: This shows nature as a living system rather than a resource; the diet switch moves the characters from taking from the environment to sustaining it. The story doesn’t explain this shift; it just shows it and lets the contrast make the point.

Postcolonial Lens

Everyone on the pillar has accepted the same idea of what counts as “making it”: getting higher than everyone else. Nobody questions where that idea came from or why climbing is the only path anyone considers. The Old Caterpillar, who knows about cocoons, gets ignored by caterpillars still climbing. This is what happens when one way of seeing things is treated as the only legitimate one: other knowledge gets pushed to the side until someone is forced to take it seriously.

Queer Lens

Climbing is treated as the default. It’s just what caterpillars do, and no one questions it. The caterpillars who step away from the pillar and go into a cocoon are doing something the rest of the crowd doesn’t understand and probably judges. Read this way, “becoming something better” isn’t really about self-improvement. It’s about what happens when you opt out of the one path everyone else has agreed is normal.

Structuralist Lens

The whole story runs on a set of opposites: up vs. down, climbing vs. resting, crowd vs. alone, known vs. unknown. “Becoming something better” only means anything because it’s set against staying the same on the pillar. Take away the opposition and the pillar isn’t even a bad place; it’s just a place. The story needs both sides to make either one mean anything.

Feminist Lens

It’s Yellow who steps off the pillar first and goes into the cocoon without proof it will work. Stripe needs to see her come back changed before he will try it himself. If you read Stripe as coded male and Yellow as coded female, the story hands the harder, riskier move to the female character and makes the male character the one who needs convincing, which runs against the usual pattern where a male character leads and a female character follows.

Biographical Lens

Evidence: Trina Paulus (born 1931) was raised a practicing Catholic in Cleveland, Ohio. In 1949 she gave up two college scholarships to live at Grailville, a farm community where lay women lived, worked, and prayed together. She later joined an international Grail team in Egypt, was evacuated during the 1967 Six-Day War, and lived in Paris during the revolutionary mood of 1968. Before returning to the United States in 1969, she found “Hope,” an earlier book she had written for the Grail movement, and revised it after what she had seen in Egypt and Paris. It was published as Hope for the Flowers in 1972.

Explanation: Her own life follows the same pattern as the story. Paulus stepped away from the expected path, giving up scholarships for a life built around purpose and community, much like Yellow leaving the pillar. Read this way, the book’s criticism of the crowded climb to the top makes more sense, because the story was reworked after she saw poverty and social upheaval firsthand. Her Catholic faith also fits the idea of something that looks like an ending (the cocoon) becoming new life. Her later work to protect monarch butterflies shows that the butterfly was personal to her and not only a symbol.

Personal Reflection (Reader-Response Lens)

Using the Reader-Response lens, this story lines up with something I’ve dealt with for a long time. I’m the only boy in my family, and growing up I had a cousin I always looked up to. I copied what he did, even the sports he liked, even though I was never as good at them as he was. As I got older, that turned into comparing myself to other people in general. I’d study hard just to get a good grade, mainly to get that validation, not because I actually cared about what I was learning. Because of that, trying new things has always been hard for me. I usually only do something once I see other people already doing it, which is basically what Stripe does on the pillar. He keeps climbing because everyone else is climbing, not because he has checked if it’s actually worth it. Yellow going into the cocoon without knowing what would happen on the other side is the part I have the hardest time relating to, because I don’t think I’ve ever done something without making sure other people were doing it first.
ACTIVITY 4 · TEXT, CONTEXT, READER

Anna Karenina (Film Analysis)

Film directed by Joe Wright, based on the novel by Leo Tolstoy

1. While watching, look closely at words, structure, imagery, symbolism, and patterns.

Joe Wright stages much of the film inside a theater, showing that Russian high society is a performance where everyone plays a role and is watched and judged. Recurring symbols like the train, which marks Anna’s first meeting with Vronsky and her death, and the officials stamping papers show how modern life and rigid rules crush individuals who step outside the script.

2. Connect the movie to history, culture, and society according to the settings of the film.

Set in 1870s Imperial Russia, the film shows an aristocracy clinging to strict rules of reputation, marriage, and religion while railways and modern change arrive. Society forgives Stiva’s affair but shuns Anna for hers, exposing a gender double standard in which women had little legal or social independence, and divorce was almost impossible.

3. Find the significance of the story to the present contemporary life of the people around the world.

Anna’s public shaming resembles today’s social media pile-ons and cancel culture, and women are still judged more harshly than men for the same behavior. Her story also speaks to people around the world who feel trapped in marriages or roles they cannot easily leave, and who struggle to stay authentic in a society that rewards image over honesty.
ACTIVITY 5

Everywhere a Garden

A graphic memoir by Maxine Go

Reading Everywhere a Garden made me think about the part of growing up that nobody can predict: the moment you leave home. In the prologue, Maxine Go’s father tells her what a fortune teller once said, that the youngest child would be the first to leave the nest. When it starts to come true, her parents wonder where she will go, whether she will come back, and whether she will know how to manage on her own. I recognized that mix of excitement and fear because I feel it too.

I also want to leave the nest and be my own person. For example, I want to go to places in foreign countries, try jumping out of a plane, and ride a sports car and race with it. At the same time, I am afraid that I am not ready. In my Hope for the Flowers reflection, I wrote that I usually try something only after I see other people already doing it. Leaving the nest means doing something without that proof, and that is the hardest part for me.

I also thought about my parents’ hopes for me. In the story, the father relies on a fortune teller’s words about his children’s future. I don’t want my future to depend on pamahiin or on what someone says will happen. I want to meet my parents’ expectations through results: finishing my studies, working hard, and showing that I can take care of myself. A prediction can say that I will leave, but only my effort can show that I am ready.

This story taught me that leaving the nest is not only about going away. It is also about proving, through what I do, that the trust my parents have in me is worth it. For now, I plan to finish my school to prepare for the real world.

PAGE 03 // MY FAVORITE MASTERPIECE

“Roland is fierce and Oliver is wise.” — The Song of Roland (anonymous), laisse 87, trans. Dorothy L. Sayers

The Song of Roland

Created by an anonymous Old French poet, c. 1040–1115

Overview & Historical Context

The Song of Roland is an anonymous Old French epic of about 4,000 lines and the oldest surviving major work of French literature. It grew out of a real event: on 15 August 778, Basque fighters ambushed the rearguard of Charlemagne’s army at the Pass of Roncevaux as it withdrew from a failed campaign in Spain, and the chronicler Einhard names Roland, a minor frontier commander, among the dead. Minstrels retold the defeat for about three centuries, and by the time the poem took its surviving form, Europe was caught up in the Reconquista and the First Crusade. The poem reflects that climate: the Basque raiders become a vast Saracen army, Roland becomes Charlemagne’s nephew and greatest knight, and the story becomes a model of knighthood, loyalty, and holy war.

Personal Rationale

Of all the works we studied, The Song of Roland stayed with me most because of Roland’s loyalty. He is brave, he is a true friend to Oliver, and when Charlemagne puts him in command of the rearguard, he accepts the post without hesitation. At that moment I thought about myself and how I can improve to become a leader who is willing to take risks. The Iliad and the Odyssey are grand epics, but this poem is the one that made me look at my own leadership, and that is why I like it more.

Themes, Symbolism & Artistic Impact

The poem is built on pride against prudence, with Roland’s refusal to blow the Olifant until it is too late as the turning point, and on loyalty against treason, with Ganelon placing a private grudge above his oath. Objects carry meaning: the Olifant is the call for help that pride delays, and Durendal is the honor Roland will not let a pagan hold. His death under a pine tree, facing Spain with his glove raised to God, shows a warrior dying as a vassal of both his lord and his God. Its lasting impact comes from survival: carried from oral song to manuscript, print, and translation, the poem endures less for historical accuracy than for its human conflicts of pride, friendship, sacrifice, and grief.

Personal Connections & Realizations

There was a time when my leader tested me by trusting me to lead my district. We had to reach a certain number of key indicators, and I was confused about where to start. I listened carefully and followed his advice: empathize with my team, listen to them and not only speak, and be a doer, not just a sayer, backing up everything I say with action. When I read the poem, it was a eureka moment, because Roland stands by his words with his actions, staying at his post and fighting to the end. Reading it helped me realize that a leader earns trust by taking risks and acting, not just by giving orders. From now on, I will keep listening to my team and make sure my actions back up my words.

PAGE 04 // SUMMARY OF LEARNING

01

Masterpieces Across Periods & Cultures

From India, the Panchatantra, the Ramayana, and the Mahabharata taught me about personal growth. Their stories talk about duty, staying calm in difficult situations, and not acting on greed. From them I learned to think before I act, to avoid acting on impulse, and to stay calm when things get hard. They made me want to become a better person.

02

Literary Lenses & Approaches

The critical lenses changed how I read. Before, I read stories only because they were required, and I did not know what to look for. The historical lens showed me that some stories carry meaning, and some help people realize mistakes made in the past. That helped me start reading and made me want to keep going with other stories. The Marxist lens showed me how people in power act and how people with less get by in their own way. It helped me empathize with people in need and see how they survived, and it opened my eyes to the fact that life is not always fair. Now I read to understand what a story is saying, not just to finish it.

03

Human Experience & Society

The works reflect human experience through duty, fear of loss, pride, and other emotions that appear in every story. In Anna Karenina, Anna leaves her children and is consumed by guilt, and she regrets it. In The Tale of Genji, Genji’s youth, beauty, and relationships all fade, which shows that life and beauty are impermanent. These stories show that the same feelings exist in people today. It taught me that people in every time and place go through the same struggles.

04

Critical Thinking & Interpretation

My critical thinking improved when I started putting myself inside the story and asking what I would do if I were the main character. That also helps me think about what I should do in real life. At the start, I would just read a story and look for the plot, and that was all. Writing the Hope for the Flowers analysis taught me to state evidence from the text and then explain what it means. I will use this to think carefully before I judge a story or a person.
